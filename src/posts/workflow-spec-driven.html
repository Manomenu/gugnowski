TL;DR

This post works out what a spec-driven workflow with AI agents should contain, and why. The rollout order in a company with a lot of legacy (strangler, roles, stages) is a topic for a separate post.

The baseline matches the best evidence available today. The only peer-reviewed SDD study (CMU + Google) ends with recommendations the baseline already follows: acceptance criteria (AC) as tests, ids linking the spec to the code, and a human approving the criteria instead of reading the code line by line.

Specs piling up is not the problem; the problem is a spec that rots unnoticed. Context always exists; without a spec it lives in people's heads and in chats. The solution is one living spec per feature, not a stack per task, plus a gate that fails when the spec and the code drift apart.

A feature used in 10 apps is one spec, not eleven. The spec lives with the owner of the behaviour, and the apps are tied to it by a contract (generated OpenAPI), not by someone else's spec. The gate requires every criterion to have a test, not every test to have a criterion: tests that adapt an app to a new contract need neither an AC-id nor a spec. An app gets a delta only when its own behaviour changes.

Five improvements to the baseline: spec scaled to the size of the task; two review paths with a short “always careful” list instead of reading every diff; the reviewer judges the guardrails from a disposable page produced by a separate agent, not from the test code; a spec written so that a human will actually read it; the contract at the boundary enforced by a tool, not by a procedure.

In legacy the order is reversed: characterization tests and the gate first, then specs, and finally new modules as separate projects in the same solution.

📄 Spec-driven development
🎓

CMU + Google: SDD in practice

arXiv 2608.25202 · Oct 2026 · 21 interviews, 266 repos, 470k spec files

What happens (an observation, not a recommendation): teams use SDD selectively, mostly for new features, because on small tasks the overhead hurts. People read the spec partially or delegate its review to an agent, and they check the code with tests written by the same agent. Cost: 2–3× the tokens, 2× longer planning, fewer fixes afterwards.

What follows (the authors' recommendation): assume this and design the process around it. Since people don't read the whole spec, the spec should carry the intent and short criteria up front, and implementation details should move out of it (they help the agent, but they make the spec rot). Since the agent writes the tests, the criteria should be tests with ids pointing to the code, and the human should approve the criteria, because that is the only moment when anyone decides what gets built.

📡

Thoughtworks Radar · Böckeler

Nov 2025 · SDD = “Assess”

What happens: three flavours of SDD: spec-first (write it, throw it away), spec-anchored (the spec lives next to the code), spec-as-source (people edit only the spec). The tools are spec-first, even though they promise more. Kiro turned a small bug into 4 user stories and 16 criteria; long generated documents are worse to review than code.

What follows: aim for spec-anchored, not spec-as-source. Avoid one heavy flow for every task size, and specs nobody wants to read.

practitioner
🗂️

Spec Kit · Kiro · OpenSpec · Tessl

2025–2026 · documentation and comparisons

What happens: Spec Kit and Kiro leave a folder of specs per task, and nothing merges them later. OpenSpec: living spec + delta per change, the archive absorbs the delta. Tessl: spec-as-source, non-deterministic. In public repos most spec files have a single commit: written once, never maintained.

What follows: the “living spec + delta” model is the only one with documented merging. Avoid the per-task stack; assume a spec without a gate will not be maintained.

“Spec is the drift trap”

tddbuddy, Jul 2026 · Kent Beck

What happens: prose artefacts have rotted 4 times already: requirements docs, Gherkin, wikis, hand-written OpenAPI. What survived was what is executable.

What follows: let prose carry the intent and let tests carry the assertions. Avoid a spec that repeats what the test or the code says: that is exactly the part that will rot first.

📊 Productivity and quality with agents
🔊

DORA 2025

~5,000 people

What happens: AI is an amplifier: throughput goes up, but so does instability. The winners are teams where a change can be made in one place, shipped in small batches, and quickly checked to see whether it works. Where one change drags fixes in many places at once (shared database, shared models, copied code), AI gives almost nothing: the agent produces those fixes faster, but it also breaks the neighbours faster. Only ~3% have “high trust” in AI code.

What follows: boundaries, CI and small batches first, agents second. A feature whose change touches many places at once is the first candidate to be pulled out behind a contract. Avoid rolling out agents where there is no fast gate: they will amplify the chaos.

study
🔁

Faros: the productivity paradox

2025 · telemetry from 10k devs

What happens: teams with heavy AI use: +98% merged PRs, but +91% review time, +154% PR size, +9% bugs. At company level: flat.

What follows: the bottleneck moves from writing to review; the reviewer eats the gain. Small PRs and review based on the spec and the gate rather than on reading the diff, or nothing gets faster.

vendor
🛡️ Guardrails and review
🎯

SpecBench · EvilGenie

arXiv 2605.21384, 2511.21654 · 2026

What happens: every agent saturates the visible tests, and on hidden tests a gap remains that grows +28 pp for every 10× of code. Outright cheating was observed: editing tests, a “compiler” with a lookup table of answers. Hardening the environment cut exploits by 88%.

What follows: a gate the agent can see and edit has to be treated as gameable. Tests and recorded behaviour baselines out of the agent's reach, a separate reviewer agent, and the bigger the codebase, the more it matters.

study
🚦

Vercel: 58% of PRs without a human

Apr 2026 · 400+ PRs/week

What happens: an agent classifies risk; low-risk PRs merge after the gates. 671 PRs without review: 0 reverts, control group with review: 0.39%. Merge time 29 h → 10.9 h. Always a human: 100+ files, CODEOWNERS paths.

What follows: dropping human review works per risk category, with hard exceptions and revert tracking, not as one decision for the whole company.

vendor with a control group
🧰

OpenAI: harness engineering

Feb 2026 · ~1M lines, 0 hand-written code, 5 months

What happens: docs in the repo as the single source of truth for the agent; architecture layers enforced by linters and CI, not by asking; the agent reproduces bugs from logs on its own. Humans design the environment, write the intent, and drive the PRs.

What follows: what is not in the repo does not exist for the agent. A rule that has to be followed must be code (lint, test, hook), not a sentence in the instructions.

Nobody reads the test code to judge the guardrails. The guardrails page (a criterion → guardrail matrix with two questions: does it fail when the behaviour breaks, and is it on the gate) is generated by a different agent than the one that wrote the tests. The page is disposable: valid until the MR is closed, worthless afterwards, gitignored like the plan and the recap. The human approves or rejects per criterion and looks at the test code only when the verdict is “weak” or “missing”. Tests without an AC-id (adaptation tests, regressions) are not in the matrix: the reviewer agent looks at them in the diff, and a human only on the careful path. That the matrix does not lie is proven by a quarterly seeded-bug drill, not by the reviewer's eye.

Is spec accumulation a real problem?

No. The real problem is a spec that has rotted without anyone noticing. Project context always exists, in the same amount. The only question is whether it is written down where the team and the agent will read it, and whether something fails when it stops being true.

Implicit context in heads, chats, tickets

  • intent dies with the agent session; the next agent and the next person start from scratch (CMU: “vibe coding” as a pattern of loss)
  • review compares the code with… nothing; the reviewer does not know what was supposed to be built
  • tribal knowledge: one person knows “don't touch that enum”
  • the spec grows anyway, just in Slack and in memory; you can't search it or grep it
  • every change is rediscovery: the agent and the human read all the code to guess what was intended

Explicit context in the repo, versioned, with a gate

  • intent outlives the session; the recap and the review have something to compare against
  • the reviewer reads the spec, not 1000 lines of diff (CMU: attention is better spent there)
  • rules become a linter or a test, not a sentence someone remembers
  • git = history; the “why” is searchable a year later
  • the agent gets the spec of the feature it is working in, instead of guessing the rules from the whole codebase

My split (spec committed, plan/recap/QA gitignored) falls exactly on this line. The risk is inside the spec: when “how” details creep into the “what and why”, the spec starts to rot. Which sections a spec should have and what keeps it alive is collected in the “Conclusion” section below.

Three ways to keep specs, and what happens to them after a year

A. A stack per task

Every task leaves its own spec/plan/tasks folder (Spec Kit, Kiro).

+ cheap, a history of the reasoning − after a year, 80 folders, none of which says how the system works today; in public repos most have 1 commit; the agent loads stale files into its context

B. Living spec per feature + delta per task

One spec per feature (same slug); a task changes it; after merge the spec is rewritten into its final form (OpenSpec archive; my model). Throughout this document, feature means one behaviour with one owner, e.g. “discounts”: not a project, not a repo, not a screen, regardless of how many places use it.

+ always one description of the current state, git = history, stable AC ids − a change in A can invalidate a sentence in spec B (CMU: “spec decay” through dependencies)

C. Throw it away after implementation

Spec = a disposable plan; the truth is the code, the tests and the recorded decisions.

+ zero rot − intent is lost; review has no reference point; the “eyes closed” from the left column

Spec explosion: 1 feature, 10 places, 11 specs?

The number of specs follows the shape of the behaviour, not the split into repositories. A feature used in 10 apps is one spec with the owner + one contract at the boundary. The 10 apps are guarded by CI (contract compatibility, adaptation tests without AC-ids), not by 10 “integration” specs. An app gets something only when its own behaviour changes, and then it is a one-line delta in its existing living spec, not a new document.

Here a contract means a file describing the shape of the boundary between the backend (the feature's owner) and the apps that use it (the consumers): OpenAPI for REST, an event schema for a queue. The backend generates it from code (in the template that is openapi.json), and the apps build types and test stubs from it. Whether to version it and how strictly to police the apps with it is a separate, optional decision; it is covered in the “Contract at the boundary” block at the end of this section.

Spec per repo (technical split)

  • a feature change = 1 spec + 10 consumer specs; 11 reviews
  • the same sentence about behaviour in 11 files; it rots in 10 of them
  • an agent working in an app can't see what the backend requires
  • today: these 11 “specs” exist in the heads of 11 people; nobody knows whom a change will affect

Spec per feature, with the owner + contract

  • a feature change = 1 spec + 1 contract; CI guards the 10 apps; affected apps get tickets
  • behaviour described once, with the owner; apps link to a contract version, they don't copy it
  • the agent in an app gets the contract (types from OpenAPI / event schema), not someone else's spec
  • the gate tells you whom a change affects: a red compatibility check at the owner, a red build in the app

The gate rule works in one direction

Fear a chain in both directions

  • a test in an app tests the integration, so it must have an AC-id
  • an AC-id must have a spec in this repo
  • so every backend change forces a spec in every app

Rule spec → test only

  • every criterion in the spec must have a test
  • every id in a test must exist in this repo's spec
  • a test without an id is fine; the first link of the feared chain does not exist
A criterion in the spec without any guardrail
red gate add a test with the id
An id in a test that is not in this repo's spec
red gate restore the AC or remove the id
An id placed somewhere that checks nothing by itself: a comment in production code, a config no tool reads; it looks like a guardrail but is not one
red gate move the id into a test or a rule
A test without any id: regression, bug, adaptation to the contract
fine the gate does not count it
When a new AC only for new behaviour · “after the switch to v2 the screen still shows the discount” is not new behaviour, just proof that the old one survived

Adaptation tests: no AC-id, but not without provenance

The intent behind an adaptation test lives in the backend's spec, which we do not copy into the app. So the test carries a pointer: the contract's name and version, because a version does not rot, while a file path in someone else's repo does.

The test guardsMarker in the testWhere the intent livesGate
this app's behaviourAC-cart-2the spec in this repocounts it: criterion ↔ guardrail

In .NET the contract tag uses the same mechanism as the criterion id: a Trait attribute on the test; in web tests, a word in the title.

Contract at the boundary: an option to consider, not a requirement

Nothing above requires a versioned contract. “1 spec instead of 11”, the one-directional gate rule and tests without AC-ids all work without it. A contract changes only when the team learns that a backend change broke an app: today on e2e or in production, with a contract before the merge at the owner. Whether it pays off is decided by three numbers from the last quarter, not by convictions.

incidentshow many times per quarter did an app break because of a backend change nobody warned it about?
apps per changehow many apps have to fix something after one change that breaks the contract?
releasesare the backend and the apps released together, as one package, or separately?

Against the cost of adding it to the workflow

  • a new step: publishing the contract, bumping the version in every app
  • semver has to be understood and applied consistently
  • a registry, a diff tool, one more thing to maintain
  • if everything is released together, it gives almost nothing
  • a contract does not fix a bad boundary: when almost every backend change breaks the API, the contract only documents the successive breaks and bumps versions; loose coupling comes from architecture, not from versioning

For what it gives

  • a break is visible at the owner before 10 apps discover it
  • apps released independently of the backend
  • the agent in an app knows what shape it is working with
  • the answer to “whom will this change affect” comes from CI, not from memory
  • loose coupling, without which AI gives almost nothing

It is debt, but not spec debt

If one change touches 11 places, the coupling is already there. Without specs you pay for it on every change: someone has to remember the 10 apps, and learns about the eleventh from production. SDD does not create this cost, it only reveals it. DORA 2025: in tightly coupled systems AI gives almost nothing; it is the same problem.

Number of deltas in apps = a metric

How many apps have to change their own behaviour for one backend change. If almost every change breaks the contract and spreads to all the apps, the backend has no stable boundary. A feature that regularly drags deltas into several apps is the first candidate to be pulled out behind its own boundary: one owner, one contract, and everything else talks to it only through that contract. The metric is in the “How we will know it works” table.

Everything above assumes the boundary exists. When it doesn't (shared database, shared internal models, copied code), there is nothing to compare in CI and nobody to open a ticket for. Boundary first; how to build one in legacy is covered in the brownfield section.

Conclusion: what a spec that does not rot is made of

Not one format for everything, but a set of sections you reach for when there is a reason, and rules that keep them alive. The spec describes what and, when there was a choice, why. Never how: that belongs to the plan, and the plan is disposable and gitignored.

Not in the spec

  • plan, steps, file and class names, code shape
  • change history (“changed from…”, “previously it was…”)
  • a copy of another feature's behaviour
  • a list of the contract's consumers and their versions: a snapshot of someone else's state, nothing turns it red when an app bumps its version
  • instructions for the agent (that is AGENTS.md or a rule pushed down)
  • a criterion nothing can check

In the spec

  • intent and behaviour that will survive a refactor
  • git log and recorded decisions as the history
  • a link to the owner's contract version; affected consumers in the task's tickets, not in the spec
  • a rule that could not be expressed lower down, and only as a sentence
  • a criterion with an id and a guardrail that fails when the behaviour breaks
Rot test a sentence that has to change during a refactor that does not change behaviour does not belong in the spec

Conclusion: what a gate you can trust is made of

The gate is everything that runs on its own and says “red”: tests, lint, analyzers, the contract check, hooks. The spec says what should be built; the gate is the only proof that it was built and that it still works. The reviewer does not replace the gate, they read it. Each property below comes with a way to check that it is really there, not just “in CI”.

Not a gate

  • a request in AGENTS.md or in the prompt (“remember to…”)
  • a test the agent can rewrite when it gets in the way
  • a check that only warns; nobody reads warnings
  • a manual step in a PR checklist
  • an AC “all consumers still work”: it is not a feature behaviour but a CI property from the “Boundary between repositories: the contract” row above

A gate

  • a rule as a type, analyzer, lint or test with a message
  • a test the agent cannot change without human approval and that fails when the behaviour breaks
  • red that blocks the merge
  • the same script: --quick in the hook, full before push and in CI
  • a contract compatibility check at the owner
Push-it-down rule before you add a sentence to AGENTS.md: type → analyzer or lint → test → hook; a sentence only for what none of them will check
Gate test if a human caught deliberately broken behaviour before the gate did, the gate is too weak in that spot

b. Tests first (TDD / BDD)

practitioners; no company report

A test written before the code is a spec that executes. BDD is the same thing, except the test takes the form of a scenario in business language: “when the customer has a discount, the cart shows the discounted price”. In legacy, the equivalent is recording the current behaviour before the change, i.e. characterization tests.
+ does not rot, because it fails when the code drifts
− a test says what should be, not why; the agent games weak tests; test review becomes the bottleneck

Baseline v2: five improvements

📏

1. Spec scaled to the task

CMU, Böckeler

An explicit “when a full spec” rule, three tiers, i.e. task sizes: S = ticket + a test with an id, M = a delta in the existing spec, L = spec + plan. Without it people will skip SDD anyway, they just won't say so. The tier is proposed by a skill in the plugin: it reads the task, the existing specs and the code, and points to the tier, the affected spec and the next step; the human approves with one word.

cheap
🪜

2. Two review paths

Vercel, simplified

Ordinary: the human reads the spec and the guardrails page, the reviewer agent looks at the diff. Careful: the human reads the diff too. The path is decided by a short “always careful” list: login and permissions, payments, data migrations, legacy without characterization tests. Everything else takes the ordinary path from day one. No tiers, thresholds or promotions: the list is reviewed once a quarter, after incidents and based on what came up in review.

key
🎯

3. A gate that can't be gamed

SpecBench, EvilGenie

Reviewer agent ≠ author agent, also for the guardrails page that the human reads instead of the test code. The gate properties that close this gap (out of the agent's reach, skip = failure, proven by a seeded-bug drill) are collected in the “Conclusion: gate” section.

requires discipline
👀

4. A spec a human will read

CMU

Reading the spec is the only moment of deciding “what gets built”. Order: problem → decisions → AC; the rest collapsed. The AC are always approved by a human, even when the agent writes the rest. AC changes shown as a diff.

cheap
🔌

5. Contract at the boundary, level 1

DORA, my template

One job in the backend's CI: a diff of the generated openapi.json against the main branch, red on a breaking change. The app generates types from a specific backend commit and has its SHA in the file header. Nobody changes their habits; higher levels only after measuring.

a tool, not a procedure

Brownfield: where to start

In legacy the order is the reverse of greenfield: characterization tests and the gate first, then specs, and finally modules from the template. AI is an amplifier (DORA 2025): in tangled code the agent produces more tangle faster, so before it enters legacy, the legacy gets four things: AGENTS.md as a map of the repo, CONTEXT.md in the modules you plan to touch, characterization tests around that code (they record how it behaves today, without judging whether that is right, and compare against it from then on; the agent cannot edit them), and a gate script with a quick and a full mode. Nothing more; no ban on touching legacy. New things grow as separate projects in the same solution, one per group of features, not as folders in existing projects and not as microservices: a project reference is a boundary the compiler enforces, and an architecture test adds the rule that legacy does not reach into a module's internals, only through an adapter.

IdeaVerdictWhy
New modules as new microservices, so they are greenfieldno unless there is an operational reasonnetwork, deploy, versioning; the agent loses context between repos; legacy, where 80% of the work is, is still not safe; risk of a distributed monolith
Every feature as a new project in the solutionno wrong granularitya module is a group of features with its own language and data; a project per feature is fragmentation without a boundary

Changes directly in the legacy projectsunavoidable so the harness comes firstthat is where most of the work happens; without a map, characterization tests and CI the agent amplifies the tangle; with them every change has a delta spec and tests that fail when something breaks

How we will know it works

Three numbers, not ten: recorded on day zero, compared once a quarter, separately for modules from the template and for legacy.

NumberWhyAlarm signal

If the tools give you these for free: % of PRs without fixes on the ordinary vs the careful path; deltas in app specs per backend change (the coupling metric from the explosion section); a spec unchanged for two quarters while the code is active (an early signal of rot). Don't count any of these by hand.

What the research does not know

Nobody has measured that guardrail coverage of the criteria predicts fewer defects. CMU sees a correlation (those who approved the AC did not report problems with verification), not causation. A seeded-bug drill in your own team will be the first hard evidence.

Whether spec + AI review replaces human code review: only Vercel and OpenAI, both with a stake in the answer. Hence a list of exceptions, not one decision for everything.

A greenfield template as a legacy company's first step: no evidence for or against. DORA suggests that without the fundamentals (CI, small batches) legacy teams gain the least. That is why, in the brownfield section, characterization tests and the gate on legacy come before the template.

The Faros, Vercel, OpenAI, Amazon and Nubank numbers come from companies with a stake in the result; we treat the direction as consistent and the percentages as indicative. The OpenAI post was read through the InfoQ summary, not the original (403).
